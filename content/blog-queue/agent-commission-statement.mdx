An agent commission statement is the one page that shows an agent how their payout was calculated: the property, the gross commission, every deduction in the order it was taken, and the net amount owed. It is not a tax document and it is not an invoice. It is the record you point at when an agent asks why a number came out the way it did.

![A commission statement for one deal, from $13,500 gross commission down to an $8,338 agent net payout](/blog/agent-commission-statement/commission-statement-line-items.svg "Each deduction sits on its own line in the order it was taken, so the agent can follow the arithmetic without being told it.")

## What goes on an agent commission statement?

Eight things, and the order of the deductions matters as much as the amounts.

- **Who and what.** Brokerage name and address, agent name, property address, and the deal or file number so the statement can be referenced later.
- **Dates.** The closing date, and the date the statement was produced. These are often different, and an agent chasing a payout will ask about both.
- **The gross commission,** with the sale price and the commission percentage that produced it.
- **Off-the-top deductions,** such as a franchise royalty or an outside referral fee, each named and each shown as a percentage of the gross.
- **The split,** stated as a percentage and a dollar amount, applied to what is left after the off-the-top items.
- **Agent-only fees** after the split: E and O, transaction fee, desk fee, a mentor fee.
- **The net payout,** in bold, because it is the only line most agents read twice.
- **Cap position,** if your plan caps. What the brokerage has collected this cap year, and what is left.

## What does one deal's statement look like?

Example. A $450,000 sale at 3% commission. The plan is a 6% franchise royalty off the gross, a 70/30 split, a $150 E and O fee and a $395 transaction fee.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross commission | $13,500 |
| Franchise royalty, 6% of gross | -$810 |
| Splittable amount | $12,690 |
| Broker split, 30% | -$3,807 |
| Agent gross | $8,883 |
| E and O fee | -$150 |
| Transaction fee | -$395 |
| **Agent net payout** | **$8,338** |

Note what the royalty does. Taking it off the gross first means the agent and the brokerage both absorb a share of it. If you took it after the split instead, the agent would net $8,640, and the brokerage would keep $3,807 of a smaller pot. Same fee, different answer, which is why the order belongs on the page.

Add the cap line underneath. If the brokerage had already collected $9,200 from this agent this year against an $18,000 cap, the statement reads $13,007 collected and $4,993 left.

## How does a statement handle a cap crossing or a second agent?

A deal that reaches the cap is two calculations on one page. The brokerage takes only the room that was left, and the rest of the agent's share comes to them at the full rate. Say $2,500 of room against a broker cut that would otherwise be $4,200: the statement shows $2,500 collected, not $4,200, and a note that the agent is now capped. Flat per-transaction fees usually keep applying after that, so say so on the line rather than leaving the agent to guess.

A co-agent deal needs one statement per agent, each showing that agent's share percentage, their own split and their own fees. Whole-deal deductions come off once, before the shares are worked out, so they appear on both statements as a proportion. ## Who gets a copy, and how long do you keep it?

The agent gets one, you keep one, and your bookkeeper wants the figures in a form they can post. The statement itself is not an accounting entry: the gross becomes income and the agent net becomes a payable, which is the split covered in [recording commission income in QuickBooks](/blog/quickbooks-online-real-estate-brokerage).

Retention rules for brokerage records vary by state, and some commissions set a minimum number of years for transaction files. Check your state real estate commission, and ask your CPA what they want kept for tax purposes. Practically, keep every statement for as long as you keep the deal file, because the two get read together.

One thing worth building in: statements should never change after the fact. If a plan is edited in March, February's statements should still say what they said in February. ## Common questions

### Is a commission statement the same as a CDA?

No. A commission disbursement authorization tells the title company who to pay at closing. A commission statement explains a payout after the fact, to the agent. The figures overlap, the audience does not.

### Does a commission statement go on the agent's 1099?

No. The 1099 reports what the brokerage paid the agent for the year. A statement is per deal and is informational. Ask your CPA how your payouts should be reported.
