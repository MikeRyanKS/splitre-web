A flat broker fee works better than a percentage split on rentals because the fee itself is small and the work is not. One month's rent on a $2,400 apartment is $2,400 of commission, and a 70/30 split leaves the agent so little that your best people stop taking lease business. A fixed dollar amount per lease keeps the brokerage whole and keeps the agent interested.

![A $2,400 lease fee compared under a 70/30 split and under a flat $300 broker fee](/blog/rental-commission-flat-fee-brokerage/lease-fee-split-two-ways.svg "The brokerage gives up $420 of split and gets a rental pipeline it does not have to argue about.")

## Why does a percentage split fall apart on a rental?

Because the percentage is applied to a number roughly a quarter the size of a sale commission, while the paperwork is about the same. A $300,000 sale at 3% produces $9,000 of gross commission. One month's rent on a decent two-bedroom might produce $2,400. The same lease agreement, the same showings, the same file.

Then the per-deal fees land. If your plan charges a $150 insurance fee and a $395 transaction fee on every deal, those are 6% of a sale commission and almost a quarter of a lease fee. Check what your own plan's fixed charges do to a number that small before you decide the split is the problem.

## What does a flat rental commission fee cost the brokerage?

It costs you the gap between your usual split and the fee, which on one lease is a few hundred dollars. You name a dollar amount the brokerage keeps, the agent takes the rest, and the percentage never enters it.

**Example.** The lease fee is one month's rent, $2,400. Under the agent's normal 70/30 plan the brokerage keeps $720 and the agent's share is $1,680, before any per-deal fees come out of it. Under a flat $300 broker fee the brokerage keeps $300 and the agent takes $2,100. You gave up $420 on this lease. You also now have an agent who will answer a rental inquiry at 7pm, and a referral relationship with a tenant who buys in three years.

The arithmetic is simple enough to do by hand, and our [free split calculator](/real-estate-commission-split-calculator) handles the percentage version if you want to compare a few rent levels. It has no flat-fee mode, so the flat side is the subtraction you just read.

## Should a rental count toward the agent's annual cap?

Usually not, and this is the decision that matters more than the fee amount. A cap is a promise about how much the brokerage will collect from an agent's production in a year. If $300 from a lease counts toward an $18,000 cap, it moves the cap by almost nothing while adding a row to every cap conversation you have.

The argument for counting it is fairness: the agent did the work, so the money they paid you should count. The argument against is that a capped agent would otherwise pay you nothing on lease business for the rest of the year, which is how a rental desk stops paying for itself. Pick one, write it in the plan, and tell your agents which you chose.

## How do you keep rentals from distorting your reports?

Give them their own deal type rather than their own plan. In SplitRE a plan can offer types the broker picks per deal, and a rental type replaces the split with a flat fee to the brokerage for that one deal. The same type can be marked as not counting toward the cap. Deals with no type selected calculate exactly as they always did, so adding a rental type changes nothing about your sales.

## Common questions

### Does the flat fee have to be a dollar amount?

On a rental type, yes: the point is to stop the percentage. If you would rather charge a percentage of annual rent, that is a normal commission plan with a smaller base, not a flat fee, and you should expect the per-deal fees to bite.

### What about a co-brokered lease?

Record what your brokerage actually received. If the listing side keeps half, your gross commission on the deal is your half, and the flat fee comes out of that. SplitRE calculates and records the split you were paid. It does not move money between brokerages or chase the other side's share.

### Can one agent be on a flat rental fee and another on a split?

Yes, through their plans. Deal types belong to a plan, so agents on different plans can have different rental rules. A deal split between two agents has no type picker, because those two agents can be on different plans.
