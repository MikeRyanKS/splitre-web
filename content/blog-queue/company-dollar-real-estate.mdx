Company dollar is the part of a commission your brokerage actually keeps. The simple formula is gross commission income minus what you pay your agents. A stricter version also takes out money you only pass through, such as a franchise royalty or an outside referral fee. Either way, it is the figure that has to cover your rent, staff and overhead, not the gross commission.

![One 12,000 dollar commission divided into agent net, outside referral, franchise royalty and the brokerage's company dollar](/blog/company-dollar-real-estate/where-one-commission-actually-goes.svg "Only the green slice stays with the brokerage. The gray and purple slices pass straight through.")

## How do you calculate company dollar on one deal?

Start with the gross commission, then subtract everything that is not yours.

Example: a $400,000 sale at 3% is $12,000 in gross commission income. The agent is on a 70/30 split with a $150 E&O fee and a $395 transaction fee. The deal also carries a 25% outside referral and a 6% franchise royalty, each taken from the gross.

- Outside referral (25% of $12,000): $3,000
- Franchise royalty (6% of $12,000): $720
- Amount to split: $8,280
- Brokerage share of the split (30%): $2,484
- Agent gross (70%): $5,796
- Agent net after the $150 and $395 fees: $5,251

The brokerage keeps its $2,484 share plus the $545 in E&O and transaction fees, which is **$3,029 of company dollar**. The check: $12,000 less the $5,251 agent net, the $3,000 referral and the $720 royalty leaves exactly $3,029.

## Why does the simple formula overstate it?

Subtract only the agent's payout and you get $6,749 for this deal. That is more than double the real figure, because the referral and the royalty never belonged to you. They were owed to someone else the moment the check cleared.

Definitions vary. Plenty of offices do use gross commission minus agent commissions and call it company dollar, then deduct pass-throughs as an expense. That works if you do it consistently. What goes wrong is mixing the two within one set of books.

## What counts as company dollar in real estate, and what does not?

- **Pass-through costs** you owe another party: outside referral fees, relocation company fees, franchise royalties.
- **Agent payout**, which is the agent's net after their fees.

Your overhead does not come out of company dollar. It is paid from it. Rent, staff, insurance, technology and your own pay sit below this line.

## How do you track company dollar over a year?

Add up the figure per deal, and per agent, so you can see who actually produces revenue. An agent who closes $400,000 in gross commission and hits a cap early may leave you thinner than one who closes less on a better split.

Watch it per agent, not just per deal. Two agents with the same gross commission can leave you very different amounts once their splits, caps and fees differ, and a brokerage that only looks at total commission will not see it until the bank balance does.

## Common questions

### Is company dollar the same as profit?

No. It is revenue to the brokerage before overhead. Subtract rent, staff, insurance and technology to get something closer to profit.

### Does a capped agent still generate company dollar?

Yes, through flat fees. Once an agent is capped you stop collecting a percentage, but a transaction fee or E&O fee on each deal still counts. See [the commission cap explained](/blog/real-estate-commission-cap-explained) for how that works.

### Where do I record it in QuickBooks?

Record gross commission as income and the agent payout as an expense. Your accountant decides how pass-throughs are booked, so confirm that with them. The [free commission split calculator](/real-estate-commission-split-calculator) is a quick way to test a deal before you enter it.
