An E&O fee is what the brokerage charges an agent toward errors and omissions insurance, and nearly every commission plan has one. Most brokerages charge a flat amount per closed deal. Some charge a percentage instead. The percentage is of the agent's own share after the split, not of the gross commission, and that one distinction decides who really pays.

![A $15,000 commission split into a $4,500 broker cut and a $10,500 agent share, then a flat $150 fee against 2%](/blog/eo-fee-commission-plan/where-eo-fee-is-deducted.svg "A flat fee and a percentage fee are both taken from the agent's share, so a bigger deal changes only one of them.")

## Where does an E&O fee come out of a commission plan?

After the split, from the agent's side. That puts it in a different group from a franchise royalty or an outside referral fee, which come off the gross commission before anything is divided. The grouping matters because it decides who absorbs the cost.

Take a fee off the top and the brokerage carries part of it, in proportion to the split. Take it off the agent's share and the agent carries all of it. An E&O fee belongs in the second group, because it is a charge to the agent for coverage the brokerage arranges, not a cost of the transaction itself.

So when a plan says E&O is 2%, the sensible reading is 2% of the agent's share. On a $10,500 agent share that is $210, not 2% of a much larger pre-split figure. Get that wrong in a spreadsheet and the fee roughly triples on a 70/30 plan.

## Flat fee or a percentage of the agent's share?

A flat fee is the same on every closing. A percentage rises with the deal. The difference only looks small until you put a cheap deal and an expensive one side by side.

Example. A 70/30 plan, no franchise fee, comparing a flat $150 against 2% of the agent's share:

| Gross commission | Agent share | Flat $150 | 2% of share |
|---|---|---|---|
| $4,000 | $2,800 | $150 | $56 |
| $15,000 | $10,500 | $150 | $210 |
| $30,000 | $21,000 | $150 | $420 |

On the small deal the flat fee is 5.4% of what the agent takes home. On the big one it is 0.7%. The percentage fee does the opposite, which is why it reads as fairer to agents working lower price bands and worse to your top producers.

Over a year it evens out less than brokers expect. An agent closing 12 deals with an average agent share of $10,500 pays $1,800 under the flat fee and $2,520 under the 2% version. The percentage collects more from exactly the people you most want to keep.

## Should the E&O fee stop at a yearly amount?

A fee can carry its own annual ceiling, separate from the commission cap. On a flat $150 with a $750 yearly limit, the agent pays it on five closings and nothing after that.

That is a real recruiting line for a producer, and it is a real cost to you. Your own insurance premium does not stop at an agent's fifth deal. If you set a ceiling, set it from what your policy actually costs per agent, not from what sounds generous.

Some brokerages charge nothing and absorb E&O entirely. That is a clean pitch and a fixed cost you carry whether or not an agent closes. Between the two there is a middle path that is easy to miss: a lower flat fee with no ceiling. It collects steadily, it is simple to explain, and nobody has to track a second annual total.

## How do you keep it straight across a roster?

Put the fee on the plan, not on the deal. One plan carries the split, the cap, the E&O fee and whatever else you charge, and every agent assigned to that plan calculates the same way. The order of operations is covered step by step in our [guide to calculating a commission](/blog/how-to-calculate-real-estate-agent-commission).

Two details worth setting deliberately. First, whether the fee continues after the agent caps. Flat per-transaction fees usually do, which is often the only thing the brokerage collects late in the year. Second, what happens on a very small sale, where stacked fees could in principle exceed the agent's share. SplitRE refuses to confirm a deal that would leave the agent with a negative payout, so you find out at entry rather than at payout. ## Common questions

### Is an E&O fee the same as errors and omissions insurance?

No. The insurance is a policy the brokerage carries. The fee is how the brokerage recovers part of the premium from agents. The amount you charge does not have to match your premium, and it usually does not.

### Does the E&O fee count toward the agent's cap?

In most plans, no. The cap limits the percentage split you take, as explained in our [breakdown of how a commission cap works](/blog/real-estate-commission-cap-explained). Flat fees sit outside it and keep applying once the agent has capped.
