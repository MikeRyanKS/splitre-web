Brokerage commission plan design starts with a number, not a split. Work out what your fixed costs are for the year, divide by the agents you expect to carry them, and you have the amount each agent has to produce for you. The split, the cap and the per-deal fees are only three ways of collecting it.

![A ten agent brokerage collecting $162,000 uncapped, $122,400 with a cap, and $146,100 with fees, against $108,000 of overhead](/blog/commission-plan-design-brokerage/cap-cost-against-fixed-overhead.svg "The cap is the single biggest design decision in the plan. It costs this office nearly $40,000 a year, and the per-deal fee wins most of it back.")

## How do you approach brokerage commission plan design?

Start with the three numbers you already have. Your fixed annual costs, your agent count, and the average gross commission on a deal in your market. Everything else is arithmetic.

Say your rent, staff, insurance, dues and software come to $9,000 a month. That is $108,000 a year that arrives whether anybody closes anything. Ten agents means each has to bring you $10,800 of company dollar just to stand still, before the broker's own pay.

Now test a split against that. On a 70/30 plan with a $9,000 average commission, each deal brings you $2,700. Four deals per agent per year covers your floor. If your agents average six, the plan works. If half average two, it does not, and no recruiting language fixes it.

This is the step most plans skip. A split copied from the brokerage down the road is a number that worked for somebody else's rent.

## What does a cap actually cost you?

More than brokers expect, because the cap only bites on your best agents.

Example. Ten agents, $9,000 average commission, a plan like 70/30 to an $18,000 cap, plus a $395 transaction fee that keeps applying after the cap. Four agents close 2 deals, four close 6, two close 14.

| Group | GCI each | 30% of GCI | Collected after the cap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 agents, 2 deals | $18,000 | $5,400 | $5,400 |
| 4 agents, 6 deals | $54,000 | $16,200 | $16,200 |
| 2 agents, 14 deals | $126,000 | $37,800 | $18,000 |

Total gross commission across the office is $540,000. Thirty percent of it would be $162,000. With the cap in place you collect $122,400, so the cap costs you $39,600, and all of it comes from two people. The 60 transaction fees add $23,700, which brings you to $146,100. Against $108,000 of overhead that leaves $38,100 before the broker's own pay.

Two things fall out of that. The cap is a recruiting instrument aimed at producers, and its price is paid entirely in the margin from your producers. And the flat fee is what keeps a capped roster from being free, which is the argument for having one at all.

## What makes a plan attractive without giving away margin?

Agents compare total annual cost, not the headline split. A plan can look generous in the places agents check and still hold its margin in the places they do not.

- **Cap low, split higher.** An $18,000 cap at 30% reads worse to a producer than a $14,000 cap at 35%, and on fourteen deals it collects less.
- **Let the fee carry the capped half of the year.** A flat per-deal fee is small, predictable and easy to justify against file review.
- **Graduate the split instead of capping it.** Raising the agent's share at production thresholds rewards the same behaviour a cap does, but you never stop collecting entirely.
- **Charge a desk fee if your overhead is genuinely fixed.** We compared that against a capped split in [desk fee against commission split](/blog/desk-fee-vs-commission-split).
- **Say what happens at the cap in writing.** Which fees continue, and which stop.

## How do you check the plan before you offer it?

Model three agents, not one: your weakest, your median and your best. A plan that works on the median and loses money on the best is common, and you find out in October.

Run each of them deal by deal. Watch the deal that crosses the cap. That is where a plan either collects what you intended or hands back more. Then total the year and compare it against your fixed costs. ## Common questions

### What split should a new independent brokerage start with?

Whatever covers your fixed costs at the production you can actually expect, which is usually a lower agent share than you want to offer. Recalculate once you know your real average commission, not your hoped-for one.

### Should every agent be on the same plan?

No, and most offices end up with two or three: a starter plan, a standard plan, and something for producers. One plan per agent segment is manageable. One plan per agent is not.
