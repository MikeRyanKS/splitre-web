A franchise royalty cap stops the royalty once a given agent has paid a set amount of it for the year. A plan might take 6% of the gross commission on every deal and stop at $3,000, after which that agent's deals carry no royalty at all until the cap year resets. It is a second ceiling, running alongside the agent's split cap and counted separately from it.

![A royalty counter at 2,760 dollars of a 3,000 dollar yearly limit, with a deal charged only the 240 dollars of room left](/blog/franchise-royalty-cap-tracking/royalty-counter-stops-at-cap.svg "The deal that crosses the royalty cap pays only the room that was left, not the full percentage.")

## How does a capped franchise royalty work?

Each agent carries their own running total of royalty paid this cap year. A deal charges the lesser of the percentage and the room left.

**Example.** The plan takes 6% of gross commission and stops at $3,000. An agent has paid $2,760 of royalty so far this year, leaving $240 of room. A deal closes with $12,000 of gross commission, so the full 6% would be $720. Only $240 is charged.

That changes what is left to split. The amount to divide is $11,760 rather than $11,280. On a 70/30 plan the agent earns $8,232 and the brokerage keeps $3,528. If you had charged the full $720, the agent would have earned $7,896 and you would have kept $3,384. The crossing is worth $336 to the agent and $144 to you on one deal, and every deal after it carries no royalty at all.

Figures like a 6% royalty stopping at $3,000 are an example of the shape, not a statement about any brand's current terms. Royalty rates, caps and reset dates vary and change, so take yours from your own franchise agreement.

## What is the franchise royalty a percentage of?

The gross commission, in most agreements. It comes off the top before the agent and broker split anything, which is why a royalty costs the agent and the brokerage in proportion to their split rather than hitting one of them.

The exception worth checking is a deal that also pays an outside referral. Is the royalty 6% of the full commission, or 6% of what is left after the referral? Most agreements say the full commission, but not all of them do, and on a deal with a 25% referral the difference is a quarter of the royalty. It only shows up on deals that stack both, which is precisely why nobody notices the rule is wrong until one lands.

## Why does the royalty cap need its own counter?

Because it fills at a different speed than the split cap, and it belongs to a different party. The split cap tracks what your brokerage collected. The royalty tracks what you owe the brand. One agent can be capped on royalty in April and still be paying you a split in September.

A spreadsheet usually has one cap column, so the royalty ends up either charged forever or stopped by hand when somebody remembers. Both are expensive in opposite directions: the first overcharges your agents, and the second means you under-collect a fee you still owe upstream.

Any fee on a plan can work this way, not just the royalty. An E&O fee that stops at a yearly figure, a technology fee with a ceiling, a desk fee that ends once a set amount is paid: each one is its own counter with its own total, and each one can name who it is paid to so the accounting export separates it from your own revenue. ## Common questions

### Does the royalty cap reset with the split cap?

It resets with the cap year, so if your caps reset on each agent's hire anniversary, the royalty counter does too. Mixing the two reset rules in one plan is a reliable way to end up arguing about a December deal.

### Does a capped agent still pay the royalty?

That depends on two separate counters. Capping on the split does not stop the royalty, and capping on the royalty does not stop the split. Our [explainer on commission caps](/blog/real-estate-commission-cap-explained) covers what keeps running after an agent caps.

### How do I check a deal that crosses the royalty cap?

Work out the room left, charge the lesser of that and the percentage, then split what remains.
