Commission management sits between your transaction files and your accounting file. It owns one question nothing else in the stack can answer: given this agent, this plan and this point in their cap year, what does the brokerage keep and what does the agent get paid? A compliance tool knows the deal closed. It does not know the split.

![Four layers of a brokerage software stack showing what each layer owns](/blog/brokerage-software-stack-commission-management/what-each-layer-owns.svg "Most small brokerages already run three of these four layers, with the third one living in a spreadsheet.")

## What belongs in a brokerage software stack?

Four layers, and for a 5 to 50 agent office they rarely come from one vendor.

- **A CRM** for leads, contacts and follow up, which is everything before there is a deal.
- **Transaction management and compliance** for documents, signatures, checklists and deadlines. This is the layer your state licensing audit cares about.
- **Commission management** for splits, caps, fees and the payout figure.
- **Accounting** for the ledger, the bills and the filings.

Suites exist that cover two or three of these, and some brokerages are happy with that. The trade-off is familiar: one login and one bill, against a compliance module you would not have chosen on its own.

## Why does commission management sit on its own?

Because it is the only layer that has to carry a running total across the year. Documents are per deal. Ledger entries are per month. A cap is cumulative, and the arithmetic on any given deal depends on every deal before it.

**Example.** An agent is on 80/20 against a $16,000 cap and your brokerage has collected $14,800 from her. Her next closing produces $10,000 of gross commission. A flat 20% would be $2,000, but only $1,200 of room is left, so the brokerage collects $1,200 and she keeps $8,800. She is capped from that moment, and her plan's $250 post-cap transaction fee applies, so her payout is $8,550.

No document tool in the stack has the $14,800. Neither does the accounting file, which only sees the entries after the figure has been decided. That is the gap a spreadsheet usually fills, and the deal that crosses the cap is the one it gets wrong.

## Common questions

### Can a transaction management tool handle commissions too?

Some advertise it, and for a flat split with no cap it will usually be fine. Ask to see a capped agent's deal that straddles the limit, a co-agent transaction, and a plan edit, then check whether deals already closed keep the numbers they were recorded with. That last one matters more than it sounds.

### What if we already run a suite?

Then the question is whether its commission module handles your plans, not whether it exists. Brokerages with anniversary caps, capped fees, team pools or a split between two companies tend to be the ones that outgrow a bundled module.
