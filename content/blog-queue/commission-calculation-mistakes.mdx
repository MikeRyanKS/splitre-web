Most commission calculation mistakes are not arithmetic errors. They are structural: a fee taken in the wrong order, a deal counted against the wrong year, a cap crossing charged in full. A spreadsheet will carry out every one of them without complaint, because a sheet does what the formula says rather than what the plan says.

![A 6% royalty taken off the gross leaves the agent $8,883, while taking it after the split leaves $8,640](/blog/commission-calculation-mistakes/right-and-wrong-fee-order.svg "The fee is identical in both columns. Only its position in the calculation changed, and $243 moved with it.")

## Which commission calculation mistakes come from the order of the math?

Three of the seven, and they survive longest because every row looks plausible.

**1. A franchise royalty taken after the split.** The royalty comes off the gross commission first, then what is left is split. On a $13,500 commission with a 6% royalty and a 70/30 plan, doing it in the right order leaves the agent $8,883. Taking the royalty off the agent's share afterwards leaves $8,640. The gap is always 30% of the fee.

**2. A percentage E&O fee charged on the gross.** An E&O fee set as a percentage is a percentage of the agent's share, because it lands after the split. On a $15,000 commission at 70/30, 2% of the $10,500 agent share is $210. Two percent of the gross is $300. You have overcharged by $90.

**3. An outside referral deducted twice.** A referral comes off the top once. A sheet with both a deal-level referral column and a per-agent deduction column makes it easy to apply in both places. A 25% referral on a $20,000 commission is $5,000. Taken twice it removes $10,000 from a $20,000 deal.

## Which ones come from the calendar?

**4. A deal counted against the wrong cap year.** The closing date decides the cap year, not the date you entered it. A deal that closed on December 28 and was confirmed on January 6 belongs to the old year. Put a $4,500 broker cut in the new year instead and last year's total is $4,500 light while this year's is $4,500 heavy. The agent caps earlier than she should and you collect less.

**5. An anniversary cap reset on January 1.** If your plan resets each cap on the agent's hire anniversary, a January reset is not a rounding error. An agent who joined in June and has paid $11,000 toward an $18,000 cap by December owes only $7,000 more before her anniversary. Reset her in January and she can pay up to $18,000 in that window instead. That is up to $11,000 she did not agree to.

The order behind all five is set out in our [walkthrough of a single deal's math](/blog/how-to-calculate-real-estate-agent-commission).

## Which ones only show up on unusual deals?

**6. A cap crossing charged in full.** The most common expensive one. Example: the cap is $18,000, you have collected $15,500, so $2,500 of room is left. The next deal is a $14,000 commission on a 70/30 plan, so the broker cut would be $4,200. Only $2,500 of it is yours. The rest comes to her at 100%, so her payout is $11,500. Charge the full $4,200 and you have overcollected $1,700.

**7. Whole-deal deductions applied once per agent on a co-agent deal.** Two agents on one transaction means one gross commission, one referral, one bonus, two slices. Deal-level deductions come off before the slices are worked out. A per-agent layout invites the opposite, and a $5,000 referral becomes $10,000.

## How do you catch one after the money has gone out?

By keeping a record that can be read backwards. The question an agent asks is never "what is my split", it is "why was this deal $400 different from the last one". If answering means rebuilding a formula from memory, the mistake has already cost more in time than in dollars.

Three habits. Keep the closing date on every deal, separate from when you entered it. Keep a per-agent running total of what the brokerage has collected this cap year, so a crossing is visible before you pay it. And never edit a past deal's numbers to make this month's report tie out.

## Common questions

### Is a spreadsheet ever good enough?

For a few deals a year with one plan and no caps, yes. The failure points are caps, tiers, anniversary years, backdated closings and co-agent deals, which we went through in [why commission spreadsheets break](/blog/eliminate-commission-spreadsheets).

### How do I audit last year without redoing every deal?

Check two numbers per agent: what the brokerage collected against the cap, and the total paid out. Then re-run only the deals that straddle a cap or a tier boundary.
