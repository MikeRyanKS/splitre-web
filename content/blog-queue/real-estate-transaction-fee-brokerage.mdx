A transaction fee is a flat charge a real estate brokerage takes from the agent on every closed deal, on top of the commission split. It covers file review, compliance and the admin each closing creates. Most brokerages set one amount and then leave it alone for years. The decision worth thinking about is flat dollars against a percentage of the sale price.

![A flat $395 fee compared with a fee of 0.1% of the sale price across sales of $250,000, $450,000 and $900,000](/blog/real-estate-transaction-fee-brokerage/flat-fee-versus-percent-of-price.svg "The flat fee is the same on every deal. The percentage fee tracks the price, which cuts both ways.")

## What is a transaction fee in real estate, and what does the brokerage keep?

It is charged to the agent, deducted from the agent's share after the split, and it is yours to keep. That makes it different from a franchise royalty or an outside referral, which are money passing through you to somebody else. It also usually keeps applying after an agent caps, which is the whole reason brokers like it: a capped agent still generates files to review, and the fee is often all the brokerage collects from them late in the year.

Recruiting sites quote all sorts of amounts, and none of it is a market survey. Work yours out from your own costs. Add up what a closing actually consumes: the admin hours to review the file, the compliance storage, the portion of your transaction coordinator's pay, any per-deal software. Divide by the deals you closed last year. That is your floor. Anything above it is margin, and it should be defensible if an agent asks.

## Flat dollars or a percentage of the sale price?

A flat fee is simpler to explain and predictable for the agent. A percentage of the sale price rises with the deal, which looks fair on a $900,000 listing and punishing on a $150,000 one.

Example. A flat $395, against 0.1% of the sale price:

| Sale price | Flat $395 | 0.1% of price |
|---|---|---|
| $250,000 | $395 | $250 |
| $450,000 | $395 | $450 |
| $900,000 | $395 | $900 |

Over a year the gap widens. An agent closing 12 deals averaging $420,000 pays $4,740 on the flat fee and $5,040 on the percentage. Close to level. The same agent in a $200,000 market pays $4,740 flat against $2,400 on the percentage, and will notice.

The honest read: a flat fee is better for your forecasting, a percentage is better for your agents in cheaper price bands, and in a mixed market the flat fee quietly taxes the small deals hardest. Pick one and publish it. Agents comparing offices add your fee to your split and compare the total, so a fee you are embarrassed to state is already costing you recruits.

## Should the fee stop at a yearly amount?

It can, and for a producing agent it changes the pitch. A fee with its own annual ceiling behaves like a second, smaller cap.

Example. A $395 fee that stops once the agent has paid $2,500 in a year. The first six deals cost $2,370. The seventh costs $130. Every deal after that costs nothing. A twelve-deal agent pays $2,500 instead of $4,740, and she can work that out on the back of a napkin before she signs.

The trade-off is yours: your admin cost per closing does not stop at deal seven. Cap the fee and you are betting that retaining a producer is worth more than the fee you gave up. That is often true. It is still a bet.

## How do you disclose and record it?

Put the fee in the agent's independent contractor agreement: the amount, when it applies, and whether it continues after the cap. Then put it on the commission statement as its own named line. An unexplained deduction is the fastest route to a payout dispute, which costs more than the fee.

If any part of the fee is charged to the buyer or seller rather than the agent, that is a different question with consumer disclosure rules attached, and it has been litigated. Ask your attorney and your state real estate commission before you add a fee to a closing statement. Nothing here is legal advice.

On the bookkeeping side the fee is brokerage revenue, so it belongs with your company dollar rather than with pass-through costs. ## Common questions

### Does a transaction fee count toward the agent's cap?

Usually not, and in most plans it should not. The cap limits the percentage you take from each deal, which is covered in our [guide to how a commission cap works](/blog/real-estate-commission-cap-explained). A flat fee sits outside it and keeps applying once the agent caps.

### Transaction fee or a higher split?

A higher split collects more from producers and nothing from an agent who does not close. A fee collects the same from everyone who closes. If your overhead is fixed, lean on the fee or a desk charge, which we compared in [desk fee against a capped split](/blog/desk-fee-vs-commission-split).

### How do I test a fee before I announce it?

Run your average deal and your smallest deal through the [free split calculator](/real-estate-commission-split-calculator) with the fee in place, and look at the agent's net on the small one. If it reads badly, your agents will see it too.
