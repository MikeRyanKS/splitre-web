A real estate commission split example is only useful if it shows the whole deal, not just the percentage. Eight plan shapes are below, each run against the same $500,000 sale at 3%, which is $15,000 of gross commission. The split percentage is one line in the middle. What comes off before it, and what comes off after, decides what the agent actually banks.

![A $15,000 commission split in four stages down to an agent net of $9,325](/blog/commission-split-examples/three-stages-of-one-deal.svg "Off the top first, then the split, then the agent's own fees. Reversing any two of those changes the payout.")

## What does a basic real estate commission split example look like?

Start with nothing in the way. A 70/30 plan on $15,000 of gross commission gives the brokerage $4,500 and the agent $10,500, and that is the version every recruiting page shows. Almost nobody is on it, because a real plan has a cap, a fee or two, and something that comes off the top first.

The order matters more than the percentages. Off-the-top items, a franchise royalty or an outside referral, come out of the gross first. The split applies to what is left. Agent-only charges come out of the agent's share after that. The three stages are the same on every plan, and [the stage by stage walkthrough](/blog/how-to-calculate-real-estate-agent-commission) sets out why reversing two of them changes the answer.

**Example.** Same $15,000, but the plan takes a 6% franchise royalty and charges $150 for insurance and a $395 transaction fee. The royalty is $900, so $14,100 is splittable. At 70/30 the brokerage's cut is $4,230 and the agent's share is $9,870. The two agent fees come out of that share, leaving the agent $9,325. The brokerage ends with $4,230 of split plus $545 of fees. The $900 royalty was never its money.

## How do a cap and a few fees change the same deal?

They change it by thousands, usually in the agent's favor late in the year. Here are all eight shapes against that one $15,000 commission.

| Plan | Brokerage keeps | Agent takes home |
|---|---|---|
| Flat 70/30, no cap | $4,500 | $10,500 |
| 70/30 with $1,500 of cap room left | $1,500 | $13,500 |
| Graduated 70/30 to $50,000 of GCI then 80/20, agent at $47,000 | $3,300 | $11,700 |
| 90/10 with a $500 desk fee per deal | $2,000 | $13,000 |
| 6% royalty, 70/30, $150 insurance and $395 transaction fee | $4,230 plus $545 of fees | $9,325 |
| 25% outside referral, then 70/30 | $3,375 | $7,875 |
| Agent already capped, $250 post cap fee | $250 | $14,750 |
| Co-agent deal, 60/40 shares, both on 70/30 | $4,500 | $6,300 and $4,200 |

Two rows deserve a second look. The cap row is the one a spreadsheet gets wrong: the agent had already paid $16,500 toward an $18,000 cap, so the brokerage collects only the $1,500 of room that is left, not its usual $4,500. [What a cap does on the deal that crosses it](/blog/real-estate-commission-cap-explained) is the most expensive thing on this page to get wrong. The graduated row splits one deal across two tiers, $3,000 of it at 70/30 and the other $12,000 at 80/20.

## What happens when two agents are on one deal?

Each agent's share runs through their own plan and their own cap. On the co-agent row above, the listing agent takes a 60% share, so $9,000 of personal gross commission, and her 70/30 split hands the brokerage $2,700 and keeps her $6,300. The other agent's 40% is $6,000, which gives the brokerage $1,800 and him $4,200. The brokerage still collects $4,500 in total, but it is drawn from two separate cap balances, and one agent capping out does not change what the other takes home.

## Which of these should you actually write down?

The fewest shapes that describe how you really pay people. Most brokerages need two or three plans, not eight, and a per-agent override for the one senior agent on a different split. Writing a plan for every exception is how a plan library gets to eleven entries nobody can explain. The [plan setup guide](/docs/creating-commission-plans) walks through the fields in the order the money moves.

Whatever you pick, write down the order of operations too, and keep a worked example of a cap crossing next to it. That one case is where last year's numbers and this year's disagree.

## Common questions

### Does an agent still pay fees after they cap?

Almost always. A cap limits the percentage split, not the flat per-deal charges. In the seventh row above the split is zero and the agent still pays $250, which is why a capped agent's deal is worth $14,750 to them rather than the full $15,000.

### Is the franchise royalty part of our revenue?

No. A royalty and an outside referral are money passing through the brokerage to someone else, so they do not belong in company dollar. In the worked example the brokerage's revenue is $4,775, not $5,675.
