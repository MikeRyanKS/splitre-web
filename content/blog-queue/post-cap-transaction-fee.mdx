A post cap transaction fee is the flat amount a brokerage keeps charging per deal once an agent has reached their annual cap. The percentage split stops. The fee does not. It is how an independent real estate brokerage keeps covering the cost of a transaction after it has given up its share of the commission on that agent's deals.

![The same $15,000 deal before and after the cap, a $4,500 split against a $250 flat fee](/blog/post-cap-transaction-fee/before-and-after-the-cap.svg "After the cap the brokerage's income on a deal falls from a four figure split to a flat fee, while the work on the file stays the same.")

## How does a post cap transaction fee work at a real estate brokerage?

The cap ends the split and nothing else. Once the brokerage has collected its cap amount from an agent in a cap year, that agent keeps 100% of the commission on every deal for the rest of the year, minus any flat charges the plan says continue. The transaction fee is the usual one. Insurance fees often continue too.

A plan can charge a different amount after the cap than before it. A $395 transaction fee before the cap can become a $250 post cap fee, or the other way round. The honest version of the argument to an agent is simple: your deals cost us the same to process in November as they did in March, and the split is no longer paying for that.

**Example.** An agent on a 70/30 plan reaches an $18,000 cap in August. She closes eight more deals that year, each with $15,000 of gross commission. Before the cap, the brokerage's 30% on those eight deals would have been $36,000. After it, with a $250 post cap fee, the brokerage collects $2,000 across all eight, and she takes home $14,750 a deal instead of $10,500. That gap is the whole reason agents ask about caps in a recruiting conversation.

## Why would a brokerage let the fee step down?

Because a high producer would otherwise pay the fee thirty times. A step down puts a second ceiling on the flat fees, so the agent's total cost for the year is bounded twice: once by the cap on the split, once by the fee schedule.

The shape is a starting amount per deal until a running total is reached, then a lower amount for the rest of the year. A plan like $250 a deal until $5,000 of those fees has been paid, then $100, means a twenty-deal post cap run costs $5,000 and the twenty-first deal costs $100. An agent closing 24 deals after capping pays $5,400 rather than $6,000. The brokerage gives up a few hundred dollars and gets a number its best agent can quote without resentment.

## What should you charge, and what does the cap really stop?

Charge what the file costs you, and be clear which fees survive the cap. A per-deal fee that covers compliance review, the transaction coordinator's time and the broker's own liability is defensible. A fee that exists because the split stopped is the kind of thing agents compare notes about.

Write the fee into the plan rather than adding it by hand per deal, and decide separately whether the fee itself has a yearly ceiling. Any fee on a plan can name who it is paid to and stop after a set annual amount, which is how a royalty that ends at $3,000 a year is tracked. Guidance for both sits in [the cap setup guide](/docs/setting-up-agent-caps). For the arithmetic on the deal where the split and the fee change places, see [what a cap does on the deal that crosses it](/blog/real-estate-commission-cap-explained).

One thing to be plain about: this is calculation and record keeping, not collection. The fee comes out of the agent's payout in the breakdown. Nothing bills the agent, and nothing moves money between accounts.

## Common questions

### Does a post cap fee count toward the cap?

No. The cap tracks what the brokerage has collected as its split. Flat per-deal fees sit outside it, which is exactly why they keep running once the cap is full.

### Can the post cap fee be a percentage?

It can on some plans, though a flat amount is more common and easier to explain. A graduated split is the better tool if you want the brokerage's share to taper rather than stop, and [tiered splits](/blog/tiered-commission-splits-explained) cover how those thresholds behave.
