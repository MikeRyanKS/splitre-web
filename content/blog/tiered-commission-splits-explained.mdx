A tiered (or graduated) commission split raises the percentage an agent keeps as their production climbs within the plan year. An agent might start at 60%, move to 70% after $60,000 in cumulative GCI, then reach 80% after $120,000. It's a different mechanism from a commission cap, though both exist for the same reason: rewarding an agent for closing more.

This guide covers how tiered splits actually work, what makes them hard to track correctly, and, since it's the honest comparison to make, how a cap-based structure gets to a similar outcome with less to track.

## How a tiered split works

Each tier is a cumulative production threshold. The split an agent earns on any given deal depends on where their year-to-date GCI stands *before* that deal, not on the deal itself.

A common three-tier structure:

- **Tier 1:** 60/40 split from $0 to $60,000 in cumulative GCI
- **Tier 2:** 70/30 split from $60,000 to $120,000
- **Tier 3:** 80/20 split above $120,000

An agent whose cumulative GCI is $55,000 closes a deal worth $10,000 in GCI. The first $5,000 of that deal falls in Tier 1 (60/40); the remaining $5,000 crosses into Tier 2 (70/30). That single deal has to be split at two different percentages, not one. It's the same kind of crossing problem that shows up in [cap tracking](/blog/real-estate-commission-cap-explained), except a tiered structure can have several of these crossings across a year instead of just one.

## What tiered splits solve for

The pitch to agents is straightforward: the more you close, the more you keep, and the reward compounds continuously rather than kicking in all at once. For brokerages competing for high-producing agents without the recruiting budget of a national franchise, a visible, mechanical path to a better split is a real incentive. It is the same competitive logic that makes an annual cap an effective recruiting tool.

## The operational cost of getting it right

Tracking a tiered split correctly requires the same real-time information a cap requires, an accurate up-to-the-minute cumulative production total for every agent, applied more often, since every tier boundary is a potential mid-deal crossing, not just one cap threshold per year. In a spreadsheet, that means checking the agent's running total before every deal, correctly identifying which tier (or tiers) the deal spans, and splitting the calculation at each boundary. Miss a threshold crossing and the agent is either overpaid or underpaid for the rest of that tier. See [How to Calculate Real Estate Agent Commission](/blog/how-to-calculate-real-estate-agent-commission) for how much room for error a single crossing calculation already has, before adding a second or third tier boundary into the mix.

## How SplitRE tracks a graduated split

SplitRE's commission plans do tiers directly. The plan editor has a "Tiered split" button, you stack as many bands as your agreement has, and you pick what moves an agent up a band: GCI so far this cap year, sales volume, what the brokerage has collected from them, or the number of closed deals. Tiers reset with the cap year, the same way a cap does.

The crossing problem gets handled too, and you decide how. By default, a deal that crosses a threshold is split across both bands. Turn that off and the band the agent was in when the deal started applies to all of it.

**Example.** A plan pays 60/40 up to $60,000 of GCI, 70/30 from there to $120,000, and 80/20 above that. An agent sitting at $55,000 of GCI for the cap year closes a deal with $10,000 of GCI.

With crossing on, the first $5,000 is paid at 60/40 and the second $5,000 at 70/30:

- $5,000 at 60% to the agent is $3,000, leaving $2,000 to the brokerage
- $5,000 at 70% to the agent is $3,500, leaving $1,500 to the brokerage
- Agent gross: $6,500. Brokerage: $3,500.

With crossing off, the whole deal pays at 60/40: $6,000 to the agent, $4,000 to the brokerage. Same deal, $500 apart. That gap is exactly the argument you will have with an agent if your plan document never says which way it works, so pick one and write it down.

The rest of the plan behaves normally around the tiers. A franchise royalty still comes off the gross commission before the split, and an E&O fee set as a percentage still comes off the agent's own share afterwards. ## Do you still need a cap on top of the tiers?

Not necessarily, and you can run both. The two rules answer different questions. The ladder decides what percentage the agent keeps. The cap decides when the brokerage stops taking a percentage at all, which the top band of a ladder never quite does unless the top band is 100%.

One tier is much easier to recruit on. "80/20 until we have collected $16,000 from you, then everything is yours" fits on a whiteboard. Three bands keyed to cumulative GCI do not, and every agent will ask you where they currently sit, which is a question your plan should be able to answer at any moment rather than at month end.

Here is the honest limit. Editing a plan never reaches back into deals you have already confirmed. Redefine a band in August and August's deals calculate the new way while July's keep the numbers they were recorded with. That is on purpose, because quietly rewriting a payout someone has already banked is worse than living with a plan change that only applies going forward. It does mean a tiered plan rewards getting the thresholds right before the year starts.
