A brokerage sends Form 1099-NEC to every independent contractor agent it paid commissions to during the calendar year, and the commission goes in box 1a. For payments made in 2026 the reporting threshold is $2,000, not the $600 figure most guides still quote. Agents you pay as employees get a W-2 instead. Both the agent copy and the IRS filing are due January 31.

![An agent's $84,000 of gross commission reduced to the $55,530 that goes in box 1a](/blog/1099-nec-real-estate-agents/agent-net-versus-gross-commission.svg "The brokerage's own split never appears on the agent's form, because the brokerage never paid it to her.")

## Which real estate agents does a brokerage send a 1099-NEC to?

Every agent you paid as an independent contractor, which in most brokerages means all of them. The test is the working relationship, not the license. If you also pay an unlicensed assistant directly, or send a referral fee to another broker, those payments usually need a form too.

Get a signed W-9 from each agent before their first payout, not in January. The name and taxpayer ID on the form have to match what the IRS has, and chasing a correction after a mismatch notice is worse than asking up front. Keep the W-9 with the agent's file so next year's bookkeeper does not have to ask again.

## What changed about the $600 threshold?

It went up, and most of the guides you will find have not caught up. The [IRS instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec) now put the nonemployee compensation threshold at $2,000 for tax years beginning after 2025, and say it may be adjusted for inflation beginning in calendar year 2027.

For a brokerage that mostly matters at the edges: the part-time agent who closed one small rental, or someone who joined in November. Plenty of brokerages will keep sending a form to everyone they paid anyway, which is simpler than deciding case by case and harmless to the agent. State filing rules are separate and do not always follow the federal threshold, so confirm both with your CPA.

## Which number goes on the form: gross commission or agent net?

The money that actually left your account, not the deal's gross commission income. The brokerage's own split was never the agent's money, so it has no place on her form. This is the single most common mistake, and it inflates an agent's reported income by thousands.

**Example.** An agent closed six deals with $84,000 of gross commission against her name. On a 70/30 plan the brokerage collected $25,200, leaving her share at $58,800. You withheld $150 for insurance and a $395 transaction fee on each deal, so $3,270 in all, and paid her $55,530. Her form reports $55,530 or $58,800 depending on how you treat the withheld fees. It never reports $84,000.

## Common questions

### Does a capped agent's 100% commission change what you report?

Only by making it bigger. A cap limits the percentage the brokerage collects, not what you report, so a capped agent's larger payouts simply add up to a larger figure on the form. The split you stopped collecting was [your own company dollar](/blog/company-dollar-real-estate), never her income.

### Do the fees you deducted reduce the amount you report?

Practice differs, and this is a question for your CPA rather than for us. If you withheld insurance and transaction fees out of the agent's share, you paid the net. Some brokerages instead report the gross share and treat the withheld fees as a separate expense the agent deducts. Either way, pick one treatment and use it for every agent every year.

### What if an agent is paid through an LLC?

The W-9 tells you. The entity and taxpayer ID on it are what goes on the form, and single-member LLCs are commonly reported differently from corporations. Ask for a fresh W-9 any time an agent changes how they are set up, and send the question to your CPA rather than deciding it from a blog post.
