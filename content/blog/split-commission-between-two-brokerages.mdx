Two different problems go by this name. One is the co-op split, where a sale is shared between the listing brokerage and the buyer's brokerage and each side pays its own agent. The other is dividing your own side of the commission between two companies, such as a partner firm that takes a slice of every deal. The second one is where the cap math gets interesting.

![A $4,500 broker share divided in order, the first $500 to one company and the remaining $4,000 to the other](/blog/split-commission-between-two-brokerages/broker-share-paid-in-order.svg "The order the companies are listed in decides who gets paid on a small deal.")

## Which kind of split are you asking about?

If the question came from a closing, it is almost certainly the co-op split. Two brokerages, one sale, one commission divided at the closing table before either office touches an agent plan.

If it came from your own operating agreement, it is the second kind. Your brokerage keeps a share of each deal and part of that share belongs to someone else: a partner company, a parent firm, a broker you went into business with. The commission arrives as one check and gets divided twice, once between agent and brokerage and again between the two companies.

These are not variations on the same calculation. The first happens before your plan runs. The second happens inside it.

## How do you split a commission between two brokerages on one sale?

Divide the total commission by side first, then run each side independently. Neither brokerage's plan has any say over the other's.

**Example.** A $500,000 home sells at a 6% total commission, which is $30,000. Split evenly, each side's brokerage earns $15,000. Your agent is on 70/30, so your agent takes $10,500 and your brokerage keeps $4,500. What the other brokerage does with its $15,000 is its business, and its agent's split has no effect on yours.

Only your side counts toward your agent's cap, because only your side is money you collected from them. An agent who closes a lot of co-op deals caps on their half, not on the headline commission, which is worth saying out loud to any agent who quotes you the full number.

## How do you split your own cut between two companies?

List the companies in the order they get paid and give each one its own rule and its own yearly cap. A rule is a fixed dollar amount per deal, a percentage of the broker share, or simply the rest.

**Example.** Your brokerage keeps 30% of the deal above, which is $4,500. Your agreement says the first $500 of every deal goes to Company A, capped at $3,000 for the year, and the remainder goes to Company B, capped at $7,000. On this deal Company A gets $500 and Company B gets $4,000. Company A finishes its year after six deals and then collects nothing more.

The order is not cosmetic. On a $1,800 commission your 30% share is $540, so Company A still takes its full $500 and Company B gets $40. On a $1,200 commission the share is $360 and Company A takes all of it. If each company should get a proportional slice of small deals instead, give them percentages rather than a fixed amount, and say so in the agreement before the first small deal rather than after it.

When one company reaches its cap, the default is that the money stays with the agent. Some agreements say the other company picks it up instead. Both are normal and the plan has a setting for it, so decide which yours is.

## Whose revenue is it, and whose cap does it count against?

Mark which company is you. Standard brokerage bookkeeping counts only your own company's share as revenue, or company dollar. The other company's share is money passing through you, treated the same way as a franchise royalty, so it belongs out of your revenue figures and in a line your bookkeeper can record as a bill. The accounting export adds a column per company for exactly that.

The agent's side is simpler than it looks. What counts toward their cap is what each company actually kept from them, so once a company has capped, the money it hands back to the agent is not counted as collected. Add the two company caps together and you get the single ceiling most brokers quote to agents: two companies at $3,000 and $7,000 read as one $10,000 cap, and the combined progress bar agrees with the two underneath it.

## Common questions

### Can the two companies have different split percentages?

They can have different rules, but the agent's split is set once by their plan. The companies divide the brokerage's share after that, not before it.

### Does a referral fee come out before or after the company split?

Before. An outside referral and a franchise royalty come off the gross commission first. [The order of operations](/blog/how-to-calculate-real-estate-agent-commission) matters more than any single percentage.
