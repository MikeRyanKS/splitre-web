A commission disbursement authorization, or CDA, is a written instruction from the broker to the title company or closing attorney, telling it how to pay out the commission at closing. The broker signs it rather than the agent, because the commission is generally payable to the brokerage. It names the parties, the property, the total commission, and every amount to be paid.

The split at the bottom happens inside the brokerage.")

## Who prepares the CDA and who receives it?

The brokerage prepares it and sends it to whoever is holding the money for the closing: a title company, an escrow company, or a closing attorney, depending on where you are. It usually goes over a few days before settlement, because a missing CDA is a common reason a commission check is slow.

The practical detail that trips up new offices is that the closing company works from written instructions, not from your agreement with the agent. It has no way to know your splits and no reason to care. Whatever you want paid directly out of closing has to be on the form.

Who may be paid directly at closing, and whether an agent can be paid by the title company at all, varies by state and sometimes by the closing company's own policy. Confirm both with the closing company you use most and with your state real estate commission before you design your form. This is general information, not legal advice.

## What goes on a commission disbursement authorization?

Every CDA carries the same backbone: who the parties are, what the deal is, and who gets paid what.

- Brokerage name, license number, address and the broker's signature
- Property address, file or escrow number, and the closing date
- Seller and buyer names, and the cooperating brokerage if there is one
- Total commission due to your brokerage on this side of the deal
- Each disbursement line, named, with a dollar amount
- Where to send the brokerage's own check, and the wire or remit details

**Example.** A $450,000 sale at 3% earns your side $13,500. The deal came through an outside referral at 25%, and your brand charges a 6% royalty. The CDA tells the closing company to pay the referring brokerage $3,375 and to send the remaining $10,125 to you.

What happens next is yours, not the title company's. Inside the brokerage, the $810 royalty comes off, leaving $9,315 to split. On a 70/30 plan the agent earns $6,520.50 and the brokerage takes $2,794.50. The agent's $150 E&O fee and $395 transaction fee come out of the agent's own share, so the agent nets $5,975.50 and the brokerage ends the deal with $3,339.50.

## How do you get the disbursement figures right?

Work from one breakdown, not two. The number on the CDA and the number on the agent's statement come from the same deal, so if they are typed separately they will eventually disagree, and the version the agent saw is the one you will have to honor.

Order matters as much as arithmetic. Off the top items such as an outside referral and a franchise royalty come out of the gross commission. The agent and broker split what is left. Agent-only fees come out of the agent's share after that. Reverse any two of those and the agent's net moves, usually by a few hundred dollars, which is exactly the size of error nobody catches until year end.

## Common questions

### Is a CDA the same as a closing statement?

No. The closing statement, or settlement statement, records the whole transaction for buyer and seller. The CDA covers only your commission and only tells the closing company how to disburse it.

### Does the agent sign the CDA?

Usually not. The broker authorizes the disbursement. Some offices have the agent acknowledge a copy so there is no argument later about the figures, which costs nothing and settles a lot.

### How do we handle a deal with two agents on it?

Treat the deal as one commission with two payouts. Run each agent's share through their own plan and their own cap, then list the results as separate lines.
